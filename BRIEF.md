# Name That Thing — Standing Brief

This file is the single source of truth for building Name That Thing puzzles. Muse re-reads it from the repo at the start of every batch; Claude maintains it and audits against it. If it conflicts with an older saved copy, this file wins.

## Each batch

- Write **10 new entries** for Name That Thing (this repo, `index.html`).
- Before writing, count the entries in ASSIGN by grade and by category. Pick the grades and shelves with the fewest entries, and say which ones you picked in the batch report.
- Only grades 6, 7, and 8 for now. Target: 10 entries per shelf, 60 per grade.
- Future levels (not yet): high school is one level, "High School," never split into grades 9–12. Its math draws on the Common Core high school categories (exponential growth, percent change over time, statistics, modeling) rather than grade-level standards.
- Until the grades balance out, weight batches toward grade 8 first, then grade 7 (roughly 6 grade 8 and 4 grade 7 per batch). Within a grade, fill the emptiest shelves first.
- Choose topics from the **Ready to build** section of IDEAS.md first, for the grade and shelf you're filling. If none fit, choose topics yourself: things a middle schooler might recognize, with numbers you can source. You may add new ideas to IDEAS.md's Brainstorm section (status `Muse idea`), but never move ideas into Ready to build; Fawn and Claude do that.
- If Fawn adds topic ideas to her message, use those first.
- Don't change any existing entry unless Fawn asks.

## Sourcing (the rule that matters most)

1. Every number in a clue, decode, math question, math answer, or story gets a link to the **specific page** that states it. No source, no number.
2. Open the page and confirm the number is actually on it. A real page that doesn't say the number doesn't count.
3. No homepages, section pages, or search pages. No Wikipedia as the source for a fact; link what Wikipedia cites.
4. A company's claim about itself gets labeled: "by the Tetris Company's own count."
5. Never write hedge phrases like "on the count behind this clue" or "in the tally behind this clue." If you can't source it, cut it.
6. Never write an explanation you can't source as if it were fact. If you're guessing, leave it out.
7. Superlatives ("largest ever," "first," "best-selling," "record") need their own source, must still be true today, and must not contradict another entry on the site.

## Clues

- Exactly 5 clues: the hardest first, the most revealing last. Test the arc by playing it like a class would: clue 1 must still give students something to reason about (a magnitude or unit that sparks guesses), never a number that leads nowhere. Each later clue should narrow the field in a way students can use.
- No two clues state the same fact in different units. No clue should make a later clue pointless.
- The number field holds only the number; the unit field holds the unit.
- Write numbers in full digits (5,000,000, not 5 million). Use the true minus sign (−), not a hyphen.
- Decodes start with the sentence, not by repeating the unit ("The distance of the race," not "Meters—the distance…").
- No clue may give away the answer to a math question.

## Clue craft (lessons from Fawn's reviews)

The goal: every clue sparks a conversation and changes the class's guesses. Clues should help, never frustrate: every unit says what's being measured ("feet tall," "years old," "the year it opened," "miles of wire") without naming the thing itself. A bare number or year with no unit is never enough. Test each clue by asking, "What would students say after this clue that they couldn't say before?"

- **Clue 1 has to give students something to reason about.** "6 years" (Diary of a Wimpy Kid) leads nowhere. "21 books" opens a real conversation.
- **Never two of the same kind of number in a row.** Two lengths back to back (the ISS's 356 feet, then 239 feet) adds nothing. Mix the kinds: size, money, time, people, speed, counts.
- **Money is a great middle clue.** A huge cost tells the class "government-scale" or "blockbuster" without saying which one (the ISS's $150 billion).
- **Don't give it away early.** "Coral" at clue 2 ends the Great Barrier Reef. A famous year at clue 1 ends the Wright brothers.
- **No repeats in disguise.** 181 minutes and 3 hours 1 minute are the same clue (Endgame).
- **The last clue should make it gettable,** not merely confirm what clue 3 already gave away.
- **No bare percents.** "20%" with no context gives students nothing to argue about; put the share in the math instead, or give it a unit with context ("of the lower 48 states drains into it").
- **Don't stack "biggest" facts.** If every clue says "huge," the puzzle collapses into "which one is biggest?" Reach for surprising angles: people (11 tribes tied to the Grand Canyon), money, a remote point, a twist (the "peaceful" Pacific ringed by 452 volcanoes).
- **Use the units kids know.** For US students, feet and inches beat meters for heights; "77 inches" makes them convert and argue.

## Answers and grade levels

- The answer is what a kid would say: "The asteroid that wiped out the dinosaurs," not "Chicxulub"; "Wasted food," not "World Food Waste, 2022." Put formal names and years in the decodes and story.
- Only year-stamp an answer when the clues are about that specific year's edition (e.g. "The 2026 FIFA World Cup").
- Place each entry where students can plausibly know or reason their way to it. If it's too obscure for grade 6, put it in grade 7 or 8 (Krakatoa and Lake Baikal are grade 8).
- Math questions must fit the assigned grade's Common Core standards: grade 6, ratios, rates, unit rates, percents (6.RP); grade 7, proportional relationships, multi-step percent and scale problems (7.RP, 7.G.A.1); grade 8, scientific notation and operations with it (8.EE.A.3–4), linear functions and rates of change (8.F), multi-step proportional reasoning.

## Math questions

- Exactly 2 questions, built on ratio, proportion, percent, rate, scale, or unit conversion. **No one-step addition or subtraction.**
- The numbers in the questions must match the clues exactly (don't use $1.4B in one place and $1.44B in another). A question may state its own extra number if it's sourced.
- Recompute every answer and show the work. Use parentheses with scientific notation: (6 × 10⁸) ÷ (5 × 10²).
- Every answer ends with a one-line "so what" in plain words, and a period. No ✓, no pipes (|), no ALL CAPS for emphasis.
- An instruction ("Write both numbers…") ends with a period, not a question mark.
- Don't reuse another entry's question idea.
- World population is always 8.2 billion.

## Story and writing style

- Two paragraphs, about 100–150 words.
- Em dashes with no spaces—like this.
- US spelling and US dates (April 23, 2005; theaters; diarrhea).
- Never presume the audience. No "stuns classes," "surprises every class," or "students your age."
- Short, plain sentences. Check every comparison (a doorway, a school bell, a city) makes literal sense.

## Choosing People entries

- **Balance across the whole site:** people of all races, ethnicities, genders, and parts of the world. Before picking, look at who's already on the site and fill the gaps.
- **Choose people who inspired, created, or invented:** scientists, inventors, artists, writers, explorers, athletes, activists, mathematicians.
- **No one controversial.** Skip anyone whose name would start an argument in a classroom or a complaint from a parent: no current politicians, no divisive public figures, no one known mainly for a scandal.
- **Cartoon and story characters are welcome,** but only ones appropriate for middle schoolers. Never draw or show the character. The reveal image must be something generic (a pencil sketch of a setting, an object linked to them) or a properly licensed photo.
- **Real people's photos** must be public domain or Creative Commons with the credit line. If there's no free photo, use a generated illustration of something connected to them, not a likeness of the person.

## Entry fields

- Type: Place, Event, People, or Thing. Theme is never blank and must fit the entry.
- Math lens uses only the existing labels: Percent, Rates, Scale, Unit conversion.
- Every entry gets its own line in ASSIGN with grade and category. Never rely on the default.

## Images

- Use only images you can trace: public domain (NASA, Library of Congress, pre-1929 works), a Creative Commons license, or one you generated yourself.
- No film stills, posters, book covers, box art, game screenshots, logos, characters, or news photos unless you can show the license. Generated images must not copy a signature design (a famous scene, a mascot, a trophy).
- Every image gets a credit line: source, creator if known, license, or "Illustration generated for Name That Thing."
- Alt text must match what the story says.
